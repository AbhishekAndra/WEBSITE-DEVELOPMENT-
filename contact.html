Make the first conversation useful

What to include in a project inquiry

A few practical details help separate the real project need from assumptions and make it easier to discuss a possible scope.

Organization and audience

Briefly explain what your organization does and who the website or product needs to serve. If audiences have different needs, name the priority group for this project and any important secondary audience.

Problem and desired outcome

Describe what prompted the project and what would be easier for your customers or team if it succeeds. Examples might include clarifying a product offer, preparing a launch page, or making an existing mobile journey easier to use.

What already exists

Share a public website link, relevant designs, brand guidelines, content, or technical platform when available. Mention what should remain and what is open to change so the discussion starts from your real situation.

Timing, ownership, and constraints

Note any launch date, approval steps, integrations, content dependencies, or internal owners. A target date helps frame planning, but the detailed schedule depends on scope, input readiness, and review availability.

Before reaching out

Project inquiry questions

Do I need a complete brief before submitting the form?

No. A short description of your organization, audience, current challenge, and hoped-for outcome is enough to begin a useful conversation. If the project is early, say which decisions are still open rather than waiting until every detail is settled.

A more detailed estimate may require page lists, design assets, platform information, timeline, and decision-makers. The initial inquiry can help identify what else is needed and whether the project is a fit for the services available.

If some information is confidential, summarize it at a high level and explain that a deeper discussion may require a separate secure process. Please do not use the form to send passwords, access tokens, payment information, or private customer data.

Can I contact you if I am unsure whether I need design or development?

Yes. Describe what feels unclear or blocked and what you already have. For example, you might have an existing website that is difficult to navigate, an approved design without a responsive build, or a product story that is not ready to turn into page copy.

That context can help distinguish a planning, design, front-end implementation, or hosting question. The scope should be confirmed before work begins; submitting an inquiry does not commit you to a particular service.

You can also link to examples of experiences you find effective and explain what you value about them, such as clearer navigation or a simpler form. The objective is to understand the need, not to select a technical solution before the constraints are known.

Should I include my budget and target launch date?

If you have a budget range or target date, include it as planning context. Those details help frame priorities and identify whether the requested scope can be discussed against your constraints; they do not automatically guarantee a specific price, schedule, or outcome.

If the date is fixed, mention why and what must be ready by then. Being clear about approvals, content readiness, integrations, and internal availability helps avoid building a plan around assumptions that could later delay the project.

A budget range is useful context, not a guarantee of price or a substitute for defining deliverables. If you are still setting a budget, describe what must be included at launch and which improvements could be deferred so the scope can be discussed in practical terms.

Do you have a public office, and can we meet in person?

Ostacor describes its work as remote-first and does not list a public walk-in office address. Project conversations can be arranged online, making it possible to work with teams in different locations.

If an in-person meeting is important to your project, mention your location and expectations in the inquiry. Availability and arrangements would need to be confirmed directly rather than assumed from the remote-first setup.

For remote collaboration, identify your time zone, preferred working hours, and the people who need to join important reviews. Clear communication expectations help teams plan feedback without relying on everyone being available at the same time.

Is the contact form an emergency support channel?

No emergency response or fixed reply time is promised through this form. Use it for project inquiries and general discussion, and describe the impact and any relevant deadlines so the team can understand the context.

If you already have a separate support agreement or escalation route, follow the process in that agreement. Do not use an ordinary contact form to share passwords, secret keys, payment details, or confidential customer information.

For routine project inquiries, explain the issue and its impact while omitting sensitive data. If you are unsure whether a matter is covered by an existing agreement, use the contact route named in that agreement to confirm the right process.

What happens after I send a project inquiry?

The information in your inquiry provides a starting point to understand your goal, audience, current site, timing, and likely service needs. If important details are missing, those questions can be clarified before anyone proposes a scope or schedule.

Submitting the form is an inquiry, not a contract or confirmation of availability. Project fit, deliverables, pricing, and timing need to be discussed and agreed separately based on the actual requirements.

Keep a copy of the public website link and project context you shared so you can refer to the same information in a follow-up. If your requirements change, note what changed and which launch or customer need it affects.

Can I ask for a quote before all project details are finalized?

You can ask to discuss an early estimate, but its reliability depends on how clearly the pages, content, functionality, technical environment, and review responsibilities are defined. A rough range may need assumptions and exclusions to be useful.

Be clear about what is confirmed and what might change. A responsible proposal should state what is included, what is not, which inputs are expected from your team, and what events could affect timing or cost.

If major decisions such as page count, integrations, or content ownership are unresolved, a discovery or scoping conversation may be needed before a dependable quote is possible. This is preferable to presenting a precise figure based on hidden assumptions.

What if our team has several decision-makers?

List the people who need to review the work and identify one person who can consolidate feedback and confirm decisions. This helps distinguish essential requirements from preferences and reduces the risk of conflicting direction late in a milestone.

If legal, brand, security, or engineering approval is required, include those reviewers in planning early. Their requirements can affect content, integrations, or implementation, so discovering them near launch may create avoidable rework.

Ask reviewers to distinguish essential requirements from optional preferences and to return comments by agreed dates. A single person should resolve conflicting notes and confirm the approved direction so implementation does not have to guess which instruction takes precedence.
