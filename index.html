Useful before you build

A few decisions that make a website work harder

Good digital experiences are shaped by clear priorities as much as by visual design or technology.

Read more practical ideas
  • Describe the customer and the task before choosing a feature or visual direction.
  • Give every key page a clear purpose and one primary next action.
  • Write content for scanning: meaningful headings, concise paragraphs, and specifics that answer real questions.
  • Check mobile layouts, keyboard access, form behavior, and image weight before calling the work ready.
  • Choose tools that fit the teamâ€™s skills and publishing needs instead of adding technology without a use case.
  • After launch, use customer feedback and task outcomes to decide what to improve next.

Common questions

Good questions before a website project

A few practical answers to help you decide what to do next.

How do we know what kind of website we need?

Start with the audience and the action you want them to take. A clear landing page may suit one launch; a growing product or service may need a fuller site structure.

Can you work with our existing brand and stack?

Yes. We can build around existing brand guidelines and technical constraints, and identify where a change would help before recommending it.

What should we prepare before discovery?

Bring your goals, audience, current site or product, launch timing, and the questions your customers ask most often. An unfinished brief is fine.

What happens after launch?

Teams can use real feedback and performance observations to prioritize the next improvements. We can help plan an iteration path that fits your needs.

Can you help if we only have an early idea and no finished brief?

Yes. Start with what you know: who you hope to reach, what you are offering, what prompted the project, and what decision visitors should be able to make. Be open about assumptions that still need testing rather than presenting them as settled facts.

The first conversation can identify which questions need answers before design or development is scoped. Existing customer conversations, sales material, product demos, and competitor examples can all help create a more grounded starting point.

You can also bring an unfinished sitemap, a rough launch date, or examples of pages that feel clear and confusing. Those references help surface preferences and practical constraints, but they do not need to become a fixed template for your own experience.

Should we redesign the whole site or improve a few important pages?

That depends on whether the problem is limited to a specific journey or built into the siteâ€™s wider structure, content, and technical foundation. A focused improvement may be appropriate when the audience and platform are clear and a few pages create the most friction.

A broader redesign may be worth exploring when the offer, brand, navigation, and page templates no longer fit together. Reviewing the current experience and the changes you expect over the next year can help compare effort and ongoing maintenance.

Before deciding, list the pages that create the most customer or team friction and check whether they share a structural problem. If the issue is isolated, a targeted update may be easier to review; if the same confusion appears throughout the site, planning a coherent system may be more useful.

How should we judge whether our website is successful?

Choose measures that reflect what the site should help people do. Depending on the business, that could include qualified inquiries, successful sign-ups, completion of a key task, or fewer repeated questions during a sales conversation. Page views alone rarely explain whether the experience is useful.

Set a baseline where possible and interpret results alongside customer feedback, traffic sources, and changes elsewhere in the business. Outcomes also depend on the offer, audience, traffic quality, and follow-up process.

Make sure tracking reflects user consent and your privacy obligations, and verify that events work before relying on a dashboard. When sample sizes are small, qualitative research and task observation can provide important context that a single metric cannot.

Do you guarantee AI, search ranking, or conversion results?

No specific ranking, conversion rate, or business result should be guaranteed by design or development alone. Outcomes depend on the product, market, audience, offer, traffic, content, implementation, and how the experience is maintained.

We can focus on a clear user journey, responsive implementation, sound technical foundations, and a plan for learning after launch. If considering AI, begin with a customer need and assess usefulness, privacy, accuracy, and fallback behavior before selecting a tool.

Set realistic evaluation criteria with a baseline, a defined period, and a clear owner for reviewing results. If a feature affects high-impact decisions or handles sensitive information, involve appropriate domain, privacy, or security expertise before release.

What should we prepare before starting a website project?

Start with the business goal, priority audience, current website or product, and the most important action you want a visitor to take. Note what prompted the project and which customer questions are hardest to answer today. An early draft or incomplete brief is still useful when assumptions are clearly marked.

If available, gather brand guidelines, existing copy, analytics observations, customer feedback, design files, technical constraints, and a target launch window. Identify who can approve content and design decisions. These inputs help the team scope the work without requiring you to solve every strategic question in advance.

Before a kickoff, also confirm who owns the domain and publishing access, and whether any third-party review is required. Knowing those dependencies early can prevent a finished design from waiting on an account or approval that was not part of the initial discussion.

How do you approach mobile usability and accessibility?

Mobile and accessibility considerations belong in the page structure and implementation, not only in a final visual check. Useful practices include readable type, clear heading order, keyboard-accessible controls, visible focus states, sufficient color contrast, descriptive link labels, and layouts that reflow without forcing horizontal scrolling.

Exact requirements depend on the project and any applicable standards, and automated checks cannot replace manual review. Share your audienceâ€™s device needs and any formal accessibility requirements early so the agreed design, build, and testing plan can address them.

Include representative content during review: long headings, translated text, and error messages can affect layout and usability in ways placeholder copy does not. Testing with people who use assistive technology can also reveal barriers that a visual inspection will miss.

Will a new website automatically generate leads or sales?

A website can make an offer easier to understand and a next step easier to take, but it cannot guarantee demand or qualified traffic. Results also depend on the product, pricing, audience, marketing channels, reputation, and the teamâ€™s follow-up process.

Before launch, define what counts as a useful inquiry or conversion and confirm the relevant forms and analytics behave as intended. Afterward, review lead quality and customer feedback alongside volume. That evidence can help distinguish a messaging issue from a traffic or sales-process issue.

Make sure the form has a clear next step and that submitted inquiries reach an owner who can respond. If conversion tracking is used, verify events and respect applicable consent requirements; more tracking does not automatically produce better decisions.

Can you collaborate with our in-house marketing or engineering team?

Yes. A clear internal owner, agreed responsibilities, access to relevant standards, and a shared review schedule make collaboration easier. Marketing teams can provide audience insight and content approvals, while engineering teams can clarify platform constraints, integration needs, and release processes.

At the start, identify who approves scope, copy, design, and technical decisions so feedback remains coordinated. The exact division of work should be documented, including who controls domains, hosting, analytics, repositories, and ongoing updates after delivery.

Agree on how work will be shared, which environments may be accessed, and who is responsible for release decisions. Clear ownership protects your internal standards while helping outside collaborators contribute without duplicating work or bypassing established processes.
