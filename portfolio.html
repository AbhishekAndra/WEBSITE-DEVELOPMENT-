How to evaluate a digital project

Look beyond the first screen

A strong project is more than a polished image. Review the message, the user journey, the responsive details, and what your team will need after launch.

Start with the audience and task

Ask who the experience is for and what that person needs to understand or complete. A compelling visual direction should make the product easier to evaluate, not hide the information behind clever effects.

Check the whole journey

Follow a visitor from the first page through a meaningful action. Look for clear navigation, useful detail, transparent next steps, and a route back when the visitor is not ready to convert.

Inspect responsive behavior

Consider how headings wrap, images crop, controls remain reachable, and longer content flows on mobile. Responsive quality is part of the experience, not a last-minute resizing pass.

Consider what comes next

Think about how your team will add a page, update an offer, publish a campaign, or maintain the platform. A useful visual system should support sensible changes instead of making every update a one-off rebuild.

Planning a project of your own?

Can I use these images to judge a specific Ostacor client project?

The gallery images are visual inspiration rather than independently documented client case studies. They can help start a conversation about visual direction, but should not be read as proof of a named client engagement, particular deliverables, or measured outcomes.

If you need examples that match a specific industry, technology, or type of work, ask about the relevant experience during an inquiry. Any project details or results should be shared with appropriate context and permission.

Use the images to discuss qualities such as visual tone, composition, or interaction expectations, then define a new solution for your brand. Your brief should identify what evidence or approved references are available so the project conversation does not assume unverified work or outcomes.

What should I look for when comparing web design portfolios?

Look for work that explains the problem, audience, role of the team, design and technical decisions, and what was delivered. If performance or conversion results are mentioned, check whether the measurement period and context are clear.

Also consider whether the examples show responsive behavior, accessible interaction, content quality, and maintainable patternsâ€”not only desktop screenshots. Ask how the work would adapt to your constraints rather than expecting a previous visual style to be copied unchanged.

Look for clear attribution, the role the team played, and the date or context in which the work was produced. A portfolio image alone cannot tell you how research was conducted, whether the design shipped, or which business results followed.

Can you create a website that follows our existing brand?

Existing brand guidelines, design files, and approved assets can provide a useful foundation for a new website or front-end build. Share examples of elements that must remain consistent and where the current experience is not meeting user or business needs.

The work should apply the brand in a way that supports readability, accessibility, and the siteâ€™s purpose. If there are conflicts between a visual rule and a practical interaction, those trade-offs should be discussed rather than hidden during implementation.

Bring the brand assets your organization has permission to use, along with guidance on which elements are essential and which can evolve. Existing typography or colors may need accessible pairings and responsive rules before they can be applied consistently online.

How do I request a project similar to something in the gallery?

Share the image or visual direction you mean, then describe your audience, the outcome you want, the pages or product areas involved, and what you already have. Mention whether you need design, responsive development, hosting guidance, or help clarifying the brief.

A visual reference is a starting point, not a complete specification. The project still needs a tailored content structure, interaction decisions, technical scope, and responsive behavior that fit your users and brand.

Include the target audience, any must-have pages, your platform or CMS, and the person who will review the work. If the visual example is only inspiration, say which qualities you like instead of requesting a direct copy.

How can I tell whether a portfolio image shows real client work?

A credible case study normally names or describes the client with permission, explains the teamâ€™s role, outlines the project context, and distinguishes delivered work from concept exploration. Results should include enough measurement context to understand what was observed and when.

A standalone image does not establish authorship, implementation, or performance. If those details matter to your selection, ask directly for examples that can be shared and confirm whether the work is comparable to your needs.

Look for labels that distinguish concept work, stock photography, internal experiments, and shipped client projects. If attribution is unavailable for confidentiality reasons, the team should still be able to explain its role and the limits on what it can disclose.

Can you work in a specific industry or product category?

Industry fit depends on the problem, project scope, and any specialist requirements. A marketing website or product launch experience may share useful design and implementation needs across sectors, while regulated content, sensitive data, or complex integrations may require additional review or expertise.

Share the audience, sector, compliance obligations, and technical environment in your inquiry. This makes it possible to discuss what can be addressed within a web project and where another specialist should be involved.

For regulated or high-impact services, clarify who reviews claims, disclosures, data handling, and accessibility obligations. A design partner can support implementation of approved requirements, but should not be treated as a substitute for your legal or compliance advisor.

What makes a portfolio reference useful for a new project?

Describe the qualities you want to explore: a calm visual tone, clear product comparison, distinctive typography, or a simple path to a contact action. Explain which parts you do not want and how the reference relates to your audience or business problem.

This helps a team translate inspiration into an original, appropriate design. A reference should inform discussion rather than replace discovery, content planning, accessibility review, or decisions about how the experience will work across devices.

Consider bringing two or three contrasting references and explaining what works in each. That gives the team useful direction while leaving room to create a coherent solution that fits your own audience, brand assets, and technical realities.

Will you share measurable results for projects?

Only results that are available, attributable enough to describe, and approved for sharing should be presented. Website performance may be influenced by traffic, pricing, product changes, campaign activity, seasonality, and sales follow-up as well as the design or implementation.

When reviewing any claimed result, ask what was measured, the comparison period, and what other changes occurred. If a project has no public metrics, its process, constraints, delivered scope, and qualitative learning can still be discussed transparently.

Some client information cannot be shared publicly, and some projects do not have reliable attribution data. A responsible discussion can explain those limitations and focus on relevant experience without inventing metrics or disclosing confidential details.
