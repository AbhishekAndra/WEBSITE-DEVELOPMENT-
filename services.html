Strategy and page planning.

Start with the audience, the message, and the job each page needs to do.

Planning helps teams decide what the website needs to communicate before they invest in detailed design or development. We can clarify priority audiences, the questions they bring, the actions they should be able to take, and the information each page needs to support those actions. From there, the team can shape a practical page hierarchy and identify content or decisions that still need an owner.

For example, a startup with several product capabilities may be tempted to place every feature on the homepage. A clearer plan can establish a concise overview, then route visitors to focused product or use-case pages with relevant detail. This does not require a large research program to be useful; it requires explicit assumptions, clear priorities, and agreement on what the first release must accomplish. If analytics, customer interviews, or existing sales materials are available, they can help inform those choices.

A practical, collaborative process

From first conversation to a considered launch

Each engagement is scoped to its needs, but these steps give teams a clear view of how the work can move forward.

01

Share the context

Tell us who the site needs to serve, what is changing, what you already have, and when you hope to launch. Links to your current site, brand materials, or an early brief help make the conversation concrete. You do not need every answer before reaching out.

02

Agree on scope

03

Plan the experience

We align on the audience, message, content priorities, and page structure. A simple outline or wireframe can reveal missing information early, before the team invests time in detailed visual design.

04

Design and build

Design and front-end implementation proceed against agreed milestones, with feedback focused on the goals and requirements. Provide one consolidated set of comments at each review to reduce conflicting revisions and avoid rework.

05

Review and prepare to launch

Check key pages, links, forms, responsive layouts, and content before release. Confirm who owns domain and hosting access, who approves launch, and how future edits will be published.

06

Learn and improve

After launch, listen to customer questions and review the signals available to your team. Use those observations to prioritize the next improvement rather than assuming every page needs an immediate redesign.

Choosing an approach

Match the way you work to the project you have

There is no single correct way to make a website. The right approach depends on your timeline, internal skills, existing materials, and how much change the project needs.

Build it entirely in-house

This can work well when your team has the time and skills to manage content, design, implementation, and testing. It keeps decisions close to the product, but competing priorities can stretch delivery or leave specialist review gaps.

Consider it when: the scope is small, the team has capacity, and ownership after launch is clear.

Use a template or page builder

A template can be an efficient starting point for a straightforward site with familiar page needs. It may be less suitable when your story, visual system, or interaction requirements do not fit the available patterns without extensive workarounds.

Consider it when: speed and simplicity matter more than custom page behavior or differentiation.

Bring in focused specialist support

External design or development support can help a team move a defined project forward while keeping product decisions in-house. It works best when responsibilities, access, feedback, and the technical handoff are agreed early.

Consider it when: you have a clear need, an internal owner, and a gap in design or front-end capacity.

Coordinate a broader website engagement

A joined-up engagement can connect planning, design, implementation, and launch preparation when several parts of the experience need to change together. It takes more alignment at the start, but reduces the risk of treating page design and implementation as unrelated tasks.

Consider it when: the site needs a coherent refresh and you want a clear plan across disciplines.

Edge case: if the project depends on complex application logic, regulated data handling, extensive e-commerce operations, or a large custom backend, explain that early. We can clarify whether the current service scope fits or whether you need a specialist platform or partner.

Best practices

Small decisions that prevent expensive rework

Strong delivery depends on clear inputs as much as good design and code. These habits help teams protect quality and keep the project moving.

  • Write for real questions. Use the words customers use, explain unfamiliar terms, and make the primary action easy to spot.
  • Approve content before final layout. Late changes to page length and hierarchy can create avoidable design and implementation work.
  • Test actual devices and inputs. Check keyboard access, readable contrast, narrow screens, form validation, and tap target spacingâ€”not just a desktop screenshot.
  • Keep media purposeful and optimized. Use appropriately sized images and video; large assets can slow the page and distract from the message.
  • Plan ownership before launch. Confirm who controls the domain, hosting, analytics, and future content updates, and keep access credentials private.
  • Measure a useful outcome. Track meaningful tasks such as qualified inquiries or successful form completion rather than relying only on page views.
